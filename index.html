Understanding PANS and PANDAS

A plain-language, evidence-based introduction to two conditions that cause sudden, dramatic changes in a child's behavior — what they are, how they're diagnosed, and where the science is still catching up.

The basics

What are PANS and PANDAS?

PANS (Pediatric Acute-onset Neuropsychiatric Syndrome) and PANDAS (Pediatric Autoimmune Neuropsychiatric Disorders Associated with Streptococcal infections) describe a sudden, severe onset of obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) or a severe eating restriction in a child, usually along with other abrupt changes in mood, behavior, or motor function.

PANDAS is a subtype of PANS that is specifically linked to a strep (streptococcal) infection, such as strep throat or scarlet fever. PANS is the broader category and may follow other infections, immune triggers, or environmental factors — not just strep.

Worth knowing

PANS and PANDAS are considered rare, and researchers haven't pinned down exact prevalence numbers yet. A child who has had strep before and also has OCD or tics doesn't automatically have PANDAS — both are common on their own. What matters is a strep infection occurring close in time to a sudden onset or worsening of symptoms.

What it looks like

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms tend to appear or worsen very quickly — often reaching full intensity within days — and may come and go over time, sometimes flaring again with a new infection.

  • Sudden, severe onset of OCD symptoms
  • New or worsening motor and vocal tics
  • Refusing to eat or highly selective eating
  • Sudden separation anxiety
  • Irritability, aggression, or mood swings
  • Sadness, or laughing/crying that seems out of place
  • Changes in handwriting or other motor skills
  • Joint pain
  • Sudden bed-wetting or frequent daytime urination
  • Hyperactivity, inattention, or fidgeting
  • Trouble sleeping
  • A noticeable drop in school performance
A key distinction

How this differs from typical childhood OCD

Most children who develop OCD or tics do so gradually — symptoms build over weeks, months, or years before they become noticeable. PANS and PANDAS look different: symptoms appear abruptly, often reaching their worst within a few days, and are then typically followed by a slow, gradual improvement. That sudden, dramatic onset — rather than any single symptom — is what first prompts a clinician to consider PANS or PANDAS.

Diagnosis can be genuinely difficult, and there's real, honest debate among specialists about where the boundaries of PANS and PANDAS lie.
