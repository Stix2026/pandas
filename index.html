PANS & PANDAS
A parent-friendly evidence guide

When a child's symptoms change suddenly.

PANS and PANDAS describe patterns of abrupt-onset neuropsychiatric symptoms in children. This site explains what the terms mean, what is known and still uncertain, and how families can work with healthcare professionals.

What are PANS and PANDAS?

PANS stands for Pediatric Acute-Onset Neuropsychiatric Syndrome. PANDAS stands for Pediatric Autoimmune Neuropsychiatric Disorders Associated with Streptococcal Infections. NIMH describes both as conditions marked by a sudden and severe onset of OCD or restrictive eating, often accompanied by changes in mood, behaviour, sensory or motor function. PANDAS is considered a subtype of PANS associated with group A streptococcal infection. NIMH

⚡ Sudden change

The defining clinical feature is a rapid, dramatic change rather than a gradual development of symptoms. Symptoms may reach high intensity within days.

🧩 More than one symptom

Alongside OCD or severe food restriction, children may develop anxiety, mood changes, irritability, tics, sensory or motor changes, sleep problems, urinary symptoms or a decline in school functioning.

🔎 A diagnosis of exclusion

There is currently no single laboratory test that confirms PANS or PANDAS. Clinicians need to consider and rule out other medical, neurological and psychiatric explanations.

PANS

PANS is a clinical syndrome. Its causes are not fully established. Proposed triggers include infections, immune-system disturbances and other factors, but no single cause explains every case.

  • Sudden OCD or severe food restriction
  • At least two additional neuropsychiatric symptoms
  • Symptoms are not better explained by another condition

PANDAS

PANDAS is a more specific clinical concept involving OCD and/or tics, childhood onset, an episodic course, a temporal relationship to group A strep infection, and neurological abnormalities.

  • OCD and/or tics
  • Childhood onset
  • Acute or episodic worsening
  • Temporal association with group A strep
Important for parents: Having had strep throat and having tics or OCD does not by itself mean a child has PANDAS. Strep infections and tic/OCD symptoms are both relatively common in children, so the timing and clinical pattern matter. NIMH

What might parents notice?

🧠 Neuropsychiatric

OCD-like behaviours, intrusive thoughts, anxiety, separation anxiety, emotional changes, irritability or aggression.

🍽️ Eating

Sudden food refusal or markedly restricted eating can be part of the presenting picture.

🖐️ Motor & sensory

Tics, unusual movements, changes in handwriting, sensory changes or motor difficulties may occur.

What should I do if this sounds familiar?

01 · Document

Write down the timeline

Record what changed, how quickly, and what happened before the change.

02 · See a clinician

Start with medical assessment

Discuss the sudden change and ask whether other explanations need to be considered.

03 · Assess symptoms

Look at the whole child

Mental health, neurological, infectious and developmental factors may all matter.

04 · Support

Treat what is present

Symptom support and appropriate treatment can be considered while evaluation continues.

Evidence matters: The 2025 AAP clinical report states that the cause of PANS is unknown, there are no disease-specific biomarkers, and evidence for pathogenic causes and treatment remains limited. The sections below therefore label proposed mechanisms rather than presenting them as settled facts. AAP Clinical Report

A proposed pathway

Proposed1. Trigger or trigger-like event

In PANDAS, the proposed trigger is group A streptococcal infection. In PANS, a range of infectious and non-infectious triggers has been investigated. Not every child has an identified trigger.

Proposed2. Immune activation

Researchers are investigating whether an abnormal or misdirected immune response may contribute to symptoms in a susceptible child.

Under investigation3. Blood–brain barrier & signalling

Research is examining whether inflammatory signalling and changes in the blood–brain barrier could alter how immune mediators interact with the central nervous system.

Under investigation4. Brain circuits

The basal ganglia and related circuits are of particular interest because they participate in movement, habit formation, motivation and behaviour.

What does the evidence say?

What is reasonably established

  • The presentation can be abrupt and severe.
  • PANS has no single confirmed cause or diagnostic biomarker.
  • PANDAS is defined around a temporal relationship with group A strep.
  • Other conditions must be considered in the assessment.
  • Children may benefit from multidisciplinary, family-centred care.

What remains uncertain

  • Exactly how immune processes cause symptoms in individual children.
  • Which children are biologically susceptible.
  • Which biomarkers could reliably confirm the diagnosis.
  • Which immune-directed treatments help which patients.
  • How best to prevent future episodes.

Management: a whole-child approach

There is no single treatment plan that fits every child. The AAP recommends a cautious, evidence-informed approach within a medical home and multidisciplinary team. Management may address the child's symptoms, confirmed infections when present, and—only in selected severe cases—immune-related treatment under specialist care.

1 · Evaluate the sudden change

Take a detailed history, examine the child and consider alternative diagnoses. Testing should be guided by the clinical picture rather than by a universal “PANS panel.”

2 · Treat documented infection appropriately

If group A strep infection is identified, appropriate antibiotic treatment may be indicated. Long-term antibiotic use for prevention remains an area where evidence is insufficient according to NIMH/AAP.

3 · Support neuropsychiatric symptoms

Psychological and behavioural interventions can help with OCD, anxiety, tics, eating difficulties and family stress. Medication may be considered by an appropriately trained clinician when indicated.

4 · Consider specialist immune treatment only when appropriate

IVIG and other immunomodulatory approaches have been studied, but they carry risks and the evidence remains limited. The AAP recommends specialist multidisciplinary assessment for severe cases before invasive immune therapies.

About “special tests”: The AAP clinical report does not recommend routine use of tests such as the Cunningham panel to diagnose PANS/PANDAS because available evidence indicates poor specificity and results do not reliably confirm or exclude the condition.

Research is moving forward

Current research includes immune signalling, autoantibodies, microglial and endothelial responses, basal-ganglia biology, clinical trajectories and treatment studies. Parent education should keep pace with new evidence without presenting preliminary findings as established clinical facts.
