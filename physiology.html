The clinical picture

Physiology, diagnosis & management

What's believed to be happening in the body, how clinicians reach a diagnosis, and the treatment approaches currently in use — including where the evidence is solid and where it's still developing.

Physiology

What's thought to be happening

The leading theory is that PANS and PANDAS involve the immune system mistakenly turning on healthy brain tissue after an infection — particularly circuits in the basal ganglia, a set of structures involved in movement and habit-forming behavior. In PANDAS, this is thought to follow a strep infection: as the body produces antibodies to fight the bacteria, some of those antibodies may cross-react with neurons rather than strep alone, disrupting normal signaling and producing the sudden OCD, tics, and mood changes seen in the condition.

This mechanism is often compared to Sydenham chorea, a well-established neurological complication of rheumatic fever that was first described centuries ago and shares the same post-strep, antibody-mediated logic. Some researchers describe PANS/PANDAS as a milder, more purely neuropsychiatric point on the same spectrum. More recent laboratory research has looked at specific immune signaling molecules — including Th17 cytokines — and how they may act on blood vessels and immune cells in the brain after a strep infection, offering a possible cellular explanation for the basal ganglia inflammation seen in imaging studies of some patients.

For the broader PANS category, the trigger doesn't have to be strep — other infections, immune dysregulation, or environmental factors have been proposed. In both PANS and PANDAS, researchers have not yet identified one specific antibody or biomarker that reliably confirms the diagnosis.

Where the science stands

The exact mechanism remains an open question, and PANDAS in particular is described in the literature as a genuinely contested diagnosis — debated because of inconsistent findings across studies, the lack of a confirmed biomarker, and overlap with primary psychiatric conditions. That debate doesn't mean the symptoms aren't real; it means the underlying cause is still being pinned down.

Diagnosis

How PANS and PANDAS are diagnosed

There's no blood test or scan that confirms either diagnosis on its own. Diagnosis is clinical — based on the pattern and timing of symptoms — and only made after other conditions have been ruled out.

PANDAS criteria

All of the following must be present
  1. Presence of OCD, a tic disorder, or both
  2. Onset between age 3 and puberty
  3. Episodic course — symptoms can disappear then return, often more severely
  4. A confirmed strep infection (positive throat culture or scarlet fever) within about 3 months of onset
  5. Physical hyperactivity or unusual, involuntary movements

PANS criteria

Broader category, no strep requirement
  1. Abrupt, dramatic onset of OCD or severely restricted eating
  2. At least two other neuropsychiatric symptoms starting at the same time (anxiety, mood changes, irritability, skill regression, school decline, sensory/motor issues, or sleep/urinary changes)
  3. Symptoms not better explained by another known medical or neurological condition

Because tics, OCD, and strep infections are all common in childhood on their own, a careful history and timeline matter more than any single test. If symptoms have lasted more than a week, blood tests may be used to look for evidence of a recent strep infection. Clinicians also work to rule out other neurological and autoimmune conditions — including autoimmune encephalitis, which tends to progress more gradually and shows up differently on EEG, MRI, or spinal fluid studies — before settling on a PANS or PANDAS diagnosis.

Management

How it's treated

Treatment is individualized and usually involves more than one approach at once, coordinated across a child's care team.

  • Treat the underlying infection

    When a strep infection is confirmed, antibiotics to clear it are the first step and are typically the most effective way to ease a PANDAS flare.

  • Behavioral and psychiatric therapy

    Cognitive behavioral therapy (including exposure and response prevention for OCD) and, where appropriate, SSRIs are commonly used to manage OCD, anxiety, tics, and eating symptoms.

  • Immunomodulatory therapy for severe cases

    For more severe or treatment-resistant presentations, some clinicians use IVIG or other immune-directed therapies. These carry real side effects — nausea, headache, and infection risk among them — so they're generally reserved for more severe cases and weighed carefully against the risks.

  • Coordinated, multidisciplinary follow-up

    Because symptoms are episodic, ongoing monitoring matters as much as the initial treatment — tracking flares, adjusting the plan, and keeping a child's pediatrician, mental health provider, and (where involved) neurologist or immunologist in sync.

On antibiotics and prevention

Long-term antibiotic use specifically to prevent future PANDAS episodes has been studied, but there currently isn't enough evidence to broadly recommend it. Any decision here should be made individually with a prescribing clinician.

Outlook varies. Some children recover fully, especially with early treatment; others have recurring episodes or more persistent symptoms. Because guidance is still evolving — including a 2025 clinical report from the American Academy of Pediatrics that formally recognized PANS as a clinical syndrome while calling for more research — it's worth working with a clinician who stays current on the literature and is willing to say plainly what is and isn't yet known.
